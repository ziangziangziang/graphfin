\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The GraphFin landing page presents a single three-dimensional object: a
hexagonal carbon ribbon swept along the silhouette of the letter G, carrying a
travelling gold pulse. The object is not an imported mesh or a hand-modelled
asset; it is the image of an explicit parametric map evaluated on a
$(u,v)$ lattice, which makes the silhouette continuously editable. This
document (i) derives that map in closed form, (ii) reports a systematic survey
of alternative shapes against five weighted criteria --- silhouette,
photographic plausibility, semantic fit, implementation feasibility, and brand
continuity --- and (iii) recommends and specifies a successor shape: a
\emph{phase-locked closed G torus}, in which the G outline is closed into a
single loop and its cross-section is helically twisted over a
rotation-minimising frame, with two antipodal signal pulses.

The recommendation follows from three findings. First, edge crossings dominate
comprehension in node--link drawings~\cite{purchase1997}, so candidates whose
projected silhouettes self-intersect are penalised. Second, position on a
common scale is the most accurately decoded elementary perceptual
task~\cite{cleveland1984}, which argues for encoding time as position along a
\emph{closed} path rather than as a moving highlight. Third, graphene is
already a canonical object in the carbon literature precisely because it can be
wrapped, rolled, and stacked~\cite{geim2007}, so a toroidal or helically
twisted carbon sweep is materially defensible rather than decorative. All
figures in this document are generated directly from the production geometry
module, so the mathematics and the shipped object cannot silently diverge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

GraphFin is a property-graph database with native time-series support. Its
positioning sentence is a conjunction: \emph{relationships and observations,
together}. A hero visual has an unusual burden: it is neither an icon nor a
chart, but a single object that must carry a product thesis at a glance, hold
up at hero scale on a desktop, survive reduction to roughly 300\,px on a
phone, and look like a photograph rather than a render.

The object currently in production satisfies the brand requirement
unambiguously --- it \emph{is} the letter G --- and it is a genuine
three-dimensional object with a physically-motivated depth ramp. This paper
asks two questions. First, what exactly is the mathematical object being
drawn? Second, is a different object better on the combined criteria of
visual quality and meaning?

The answer to the first question is given in Section~\ref{sec:current}; the
answer to the second, together with a fully specified replacement, is given in
Sections~\ref{sec:criteria}--\ref{sec:recommendation}.

\section{The current object as a parametric map}
\label{sec:current}

\subsection{Control polygon}

The silhouette is defined in the logo's own two-dimensional coordinate system
by three ordered point sets: a defocused \emph{head} inside the aperture, a
counter-clockwise outer \emph{arc}, and an inward \emph{tail}. In the
production module these are the arrays \texttt{headPoints},
\texttt{arcPoints}, and \texttt{tailPoints}, concatenated (with duplicated
junctions removed) into a single polyline
\[
P=\bigl(p_0,p_1,\dots,p_{m-1}\bigr),\qquad p_i\in\mathbb{R}^2 .
\]
The polyline is expressed in SVG coordinates (positive $y$ downward) and then
mapped into the renderer's right-handed world frame by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:svg2world}
c_i=\bigl((x_i-c_x)\,k,\;(c_y-y_i)\,k,\;0\bigr)^{\!\top},
\qquad k=\frac{2.42}{388},\quad (c_x,c_y)=(546,470).
\end{equation}
The single scale $k$ is the only free parameter of the silhouette; it is
increased slightly above the value that exactly fits the logo box so that the
ribbon crosses the decorative breakout frame.

\subsection{Corner cutting}

Polylines have $C^0$ continuity, which produces visible facets after bending.
The production code therefore applies $n_c=3$ iterations of Chaikin corner
cutting~\cite{chaikin1975}, replacing every segment $c_i c_{i+1}$ by the pair
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chaikin}
q_i=\tfrac34 c_i+\tfrac14 c_{i+1},
\qquad
r_i=\tfrac14 c_i+\tfrac34 c_{i+1},
\end{equation}
and iterating on the resulting sequence. The operation is linear, length
preserving in the limit, and --- crucially for this application --- raises the
sequence to $C^1$ continuity at every interior junction, so the surface normal
used in the sweep is continuous. It is a subdivision scheme, not a fit, so the
design intent expressed by the control points survives.

\subsection{Arclength parameterisation}

The smoothed polyline is stored together with its cumulative arclength
$s_0=0$, $s_{i+1}=s_i+\lVert c_{i+1}-c_i\rVert$. The curve is then
parameterised by $u\in[0,1]$ with three segments, split at the two junctions
$u_1=0.16$ (head to arc) and $u_2=0.82$ (arc to tail), where $s_1$ and $s_2$
are the arclengths of those junctions:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:arclength}
s(u)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{u}{u_1}s_1, & u<u_1,\\[6pt]
s_1+\dfrac{u-u_1}{u_2-u_1}(s_2-s_1), & u_1\le u<u_2,\\[6pt]
s_2+\dfrac{u-u_2}{1-u_2}(s_m-s_2), & u\ge u_2 .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The centreline is the constant-speed reparameterisation $C(u)=c(s(u))$. The
three-segment form exists because the head, arc, and tail are edited
independently; a single B-spline would couple them.

\subsection{Sweeping the ribbon}

Let $T(u)=C'(u)/\lVert C'(u)\rVert$ be the unit tangent and
$N(u)=(-T_y,T_x,0)$ the in-plane unit normal. Rows of the sheet are indexed by
$v\in[-3,3]$. The in-plane position of a lattice site is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inplane}
X(u,v)=C(u)+v\,\frac{R}{V_S}\,N(u)+\eta(u,v),
\qquad R=0.20,\quad V_S=3.03,
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ collects deliberately small imperfection terms (sag, a localised
dull bend, and an edge lift) with magnitude below $0.03$ world units --- two
orders below the inter-node spacing.

\subsection{Depth, occlusion, and the crossing correction}
\label{sec:depth}

The anamorphic depth ramp is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:depthramp}
d(u)=\operatorname{sign}(a)\,\lvert a\rvert^{1.15}\,D,
\qquad a=2u-1,\quad D=3.15 .
\end{equation}
The super-linear exponent holds the visible mid-band near $z=0$ while both
ends accelerate towards and away from the lens. On top of this sits a
\emph{local occlusion correction}, motivated in Section~\ref{sec:crossing}:
\begin{align}
\Delta(u)&=
\exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{u-0.45}{0.12}\right)^{\!2}\right)\!(0.38)
-\,\sigma(0.84,0.98,u)\,(4.6), \label{eq:delta}\\
L(u)&=\sigma(0.78,0.96,u), \label{eq:layer}\\
\sigma(a,b,u)&=3\tau^2-2\tau^3,\qquad
\tau=\operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{u-a}{b-a},0,1\right), \label{eq:smooth}
\end{align}
together with a local widening of the sheet's layer spacing and a
corresponding scale falloff for the spheres:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scale}
\text{row spacing}=S_0+S_1 L(u),\quad
(S_0,S_1)=(0.26,0.30),
\qquad
\kappa(u)=1-0.28\,L(u) .
\end{equation}
The complete surface is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:surface}
\boxed{
P(u,v)=\Bigl(X_1(u,v),\;X_2(u,v),\;d(u)+\Delta(u)+v\bigl(S_0+S_1L(u)\bigr)+\zeta(u,v)\Bigr)^{\!\top}
}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta$ is the small depth-domain imperfection term.

The two terms in \eqref{eq:delta} have opposite jobs. The Gaussian lifts the
outer arc slightly towards the lens in the region where the inner tail
visually approaches it; the smoothstep retires the far tail of the sheet
behind that arc. Section~\ref{sec:crossing} shows why both are required rather
than merely aesthetic.

\subsection{Visibility, camera, and the focus pass}

The recycled seam is hidden by an opacity window, not by a hard cut:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:visibility}
A(u)=\sigma(0.004,0.055,u)\bigl(1-\sigma(0.945,0.996,u)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The projection is a $40^{\circ}$ vertical field of view at
$z_{\mathrm{cam}}=\max(9.8,\,6.9/\mathrm{aspect})$, looking at
$(0,0.08,0)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:projection}
x_{\mathrm{ndc}}=\frac{x}{(z_{\mathrm{cam}}-z)\tan(20^{\circ})},
\qquad
y_{\mathrm{ndc}}=\frac{y}{(z_{\mathrm{cam}}-z)\tan(20^{\circ})}.
\end{equation}
A depth-texture focus pass converts distance into defocus with a circle of
confusion $c(d)=\sigma(0.32R_f,\,2.5R_f,\,|d-d_f|)$, $R_f=2$, and --- this is
the part that matters for the crossing problem --- a sample contributes only
if it is not substantially behind the centre sample:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gate}
w=\bigl[\,c(d_s)\,\bigl(1-\sigma(0.055,0.42,\max(d_s-d_c,0))\bigr)\,\sigma(0.015,0.2,c(d_s))\,\bigr].
\end{equation}
Without the depth gate in \eqref{eq:gate}, a blurred background pixel is
averaged into a sharp foreground pixel, which reads as a hard, physically
impossible cut. The same class of artefact is known in the literature as focus
bleed, and the standard remedy is exactly an occlusion test between the gathered
sample and the centre sample~\cite{hammon2007,scheuermann2004}.

\section{Why the crossing needed a local correction}
\label{sec:crossing}

The tail of the G doubles back on itself near the aperture, so its projected
silhouette approaches the outer arc. Two independent mechanisms produced a
wrong reading there.

\paragraph{Occlusion.} The depth of the sheet is assigned by \eqref{eq:depthramp}
almost purely as a function of $u$. In the crossing region that places a
considerable part of the inner tail at a larger $z$ than the outer arc it
visually touches, so the tail is drawn in front of a ring that should occlude
it. The structural statement is simple and testable: at equal row coordinate
the outer arc must be nearer the lens than the inner tail,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:crossingreq}
z\bigl(0.45,v\bigr)-z\bigl(0.95,v\bigr) > 0.5
\qquad\text{for all }v\in[-3,3] .
\end{equation}
Enforcing \eqref{eq:crossingreq} costs a steep local depth gradient, which in
turn compresses the spacing of neighbouring rows. That compression is repaired
by \eqref{eq:scale}, which widens the row spacing and shrinks the sphere
radius locally --- the same thing a camera does to a receding object, so the
repair is visually motivated rather than a fudge. Inequality
\eqref{eq:crossingreq} is now a unit test.

\paragraph{Black notch.} A second, entirely artefactual defect was present: a
blurred contact-shadow element was painted \emph{behind} the transparent
canvas. Through the canvas' transparent background it was not a shadow at all
but a dark shape with a soft but visible boundary, which read as a hole cut in
the object. Deleting it removed the artefact without touching lighting, and the
CSS vignette that could produce a related hard edge is disabled.

\section{Criteria for a better shape}
\label{sec:criteria}

A replacement is judged on five weighted criteria, chosen because they are the
ones the current object is already strong or weak on, and because each can be
argued from the literature rather than from taste alone.

\begin{description}
\item[(C1) Silhouette and cross-scale legibility, weight 25.] Does the
projected outline survive at hero scale and at roughly 300\,px? Reducing
ambiguous crossings dominates this criterion~\cite{purchase1997}; so does
closure, a primary Gestalt grouping
principle~\cite{wagemans2012}. A shape that depends on post-processing for its
read is fragile, because the production renderer disables the entire post chain
on mobile.
\item[(C2) Photographic plausibility, weight 20.] Can a single focal plane and
a depth buffer explain the appearance? A real camera produces polygonal
bokeh~\cite{mcintosh2012}; foreground and background must not swap.
\item[(C3) Semantic fit, weight 25.] Does the geometry encode \emph{graph} and
\emph{time}? Position on a common scale is the most accurately decoded
channel~\cite{cleveland1984}, so time should be position on a path, and a
closed path is the domain convention for cyclical
structure~\cite{franke2023,rakuschek2026}. The unification of property graphs
and time series is itself an active research direction, with the two data
models historically treated as secondary attachments rather than first-class
citizens~\cite{hygraph2025,bonfietti2026}.
\item[(C4) Implementation feasibility, weight 20.] Can it be drawn by the
existing architecture --- two instanced primitive types plus a point
cloud~\cite{threejs}, a periodic node set, and a single recycled seam --- at
the existing mobile frame budget?
\item[(C5) Brand continuity, weight 10.] The product ships a G mark; replacing
it costs recognition.
\end{description}

\section{Recommendation: the phase-locked closed G torus}
\label{sec:recommendation}

\subsection{Construction}

Let $\gamma:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^3$ be the logo centreline of
Section~\ref{sec:current}, closed by a chord across the aperture and smoothed
in the same way. Let $\{T,N,B\}$ be a rotation-minimising frame along
$\gamma$, computed by the double-reflection method so that the frame is closed
by construction: with $v_i=\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_i$,
\begin{align}
n_i^-&=n_{i-1}-\frac{2\langle v_i,n_{i-1}\rangle}{\lVert v_i\rVert^2}v_i,
&
t_i^-&=t_{i-1}-\frac{2\langle v_i,t_{i-1}\rangle}{\lVert v_i\rVert^2}v_i, \label{eq:dr}\\
b_i&=b_{i-1}-\langle n_i^-,b_{i-1}\rangle n_i^-,
&
n_i&=n_i^--\langle t_i^-,n_i^-\rangle t_i^- . \notag
\end{align}
The surface is a $k$-fold helical sweep of a ribbon of half-width $r$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:helix}
\boxed{
\Phi_k(u,v)=\gamma(u)+r\,v\Bigl(\cos\varphi(u)\,N(u)+\sin\varphi(u)\,B(u)\Bigr),
\qquad
\varphi(u)=\varphi_0+2\pi k u .
}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Single seam]
If the frame is rotation-minimising and $\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=2\pi k$ for
integer $k$, then $\Phi_k(1,v)=\Phi_k(0,v)$ and $\Phi_k(u,1)=\Phi_k(u,-1)$:
the surface closes with exactly one seam, and no hand-tuned offset is needed.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{The frame does not close for free.} A rotation-minimising frame
along a \emph{closed} centreline generally does not return to itself: after one
lap the frame has acquired a residual holonomy, and a naive implementation
produces a visible kink exactly at the seam, which is the one place a seam must
not be visible~\cite{wang2008}. Either the residual rotation is measured and
compensated, or --- as in the recommended build, where $k$ is small and the
twist is a local lean rather than a full turn --- the in-plane normal is used
directly, for which the residual holonomy is identically zero.

\begin{proposition}[No self-intersection]
Let $\rho_{\min}$ be the minimum radius of curvature of $\gamma$. If
$r<\rho_{\min}$ and $\bigl\lvert d\varphi/ds\bigr\rvert r<1$, the sweep is an
embedding: the ribbon cannot overtake itself along the centreline, and the
cross-section cannot close on itself across the twist.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Why this shape is better}

\paragraph{It removes a closure defect.} The current object is an open
$C$ with a hook; both ends fade out~\eqref{eq:visibility}. Every open end is a
violation of closure~\cite{wagemans2012} and, on a rendered object rather than a
drawing, a dissolved end reads as damage rather than as depth. Closing the loop
turns a torn object into a solid one at zero cost to the brand.

\paragraph{It buys legibility without post-processing.} Because
$\varphi$ advances monotonically, the ribbon passes from edge-on to face-on
around the loop, producing an internal light-to-dark gradient that survives at
small sizes. This matters disproportionately because the production renderer
disables bloom and depth of field below 760\,px: a hero whose read depends on
post-processing is read on desktop and merely hinted at on mobile.

\paragraph{It makes the period a geometric quantity.} On a closed path,
position is the most accurately decoded channel available~\cite{cleveland1984},
and a closed quasi-circular path is the established encoding for cyclical
structure~\cite{franke2023,rakuschek2026}. One lap of $\gamma$ is one period;
$k$ is the number of phase revolutions per period. Time is no longer
illustrated by a dot in motion --- it is the geometry.

\paragraph{Two pulses set the interval, not the zero.} A single travelling pulse on
a closed loop has no internal phase reference: the viewer cannot tell where
``now'' is, which is precisely why the current hero reads as traffic. Two
pulses at $u$ and $u+\tfrac12$ fix the \emph{spacing} between updates, and the
spacing can be measured the way phase
concentration is measured in the periodicity
literature~\cite{franke2023}, and cost no extra draw calls because the signal
already walks an edge route; the route is simply traversed twice. They do
\emph{not}, however, fix a zero phase: the configuration is invariant under a
half-turn, so the two pulses are interchangeable. A zero phase requires a
\emph{fixed} mark on the structure --- a small pitch or section change at an
interior node group, reading as a terminal the object grew from --- so that a
still frame has a reference and motion only supplies direction. If the pulse
period and the twist rate are close but not equal, their beat
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beat}
f_{\mathrm{beat}}=\left|\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{pulse}}}-\frac{k}{T_{\mathrm{twist}}}\right|
\end{equation}
is a legible picture of two phase-locked cycles.

\paragraph{What closure does and does not claim.} Closing the path says the
object is continuous and the update has no privileged start. It does not make
the data periodic: a closed loop is a display of a cycle, and only a measured
periodicity in the data would license that reading. Likewise ``near'' and
``far'' carry no recency by themselves; recency has to be stated by the signal,
as in the fixed mark and the trailing edge above.

\subsection{Feasibility in the current renderer}

The recommendation is deliberately conservative with respect to the existing
architecture. The node set remains periodic in $u$, so the existing seam test
(``suppress an edge when $\lvert u_a-u_b\rvert>0.5$'') is unchanged. The
hexagonal lattice remains a single \texttt{InstancedMesh} for spheres and one
for bonds, matching the WebGL 2 instancing model~\cite{webgl2,threejs}. The
only structural additions are (i) a rotation-minimising frame in place of the
fixed in-plane normal, and (ii) running the signal twice at half-period offset.
The first is a linear-algebra change confined to the geometry module; the
second is a loop in the signal update.

\subsection{An orthogonal improvement}

Independent of shape, the desktop focus pass should switch from a circular
gather to a \emph{hexagonal} bokeh. Real cameras produce polygonal
bokeh~\cite{mcintosh2012}, hexagonal apertures are separable and therefore
cheap, and the object's material is a hexagonal carbon lattice. The theme and
the optics would agree. No geometry change is required.

\section{Limitations}

The evaluation in Sections~\ref{sec:criteria}--\ref{sec:recommendation} is a
structured argument, not a user study. Three limitations should be stated
plainly. The weights in Table~\ref{tab:matrix} are a considered editorial
choice; a different weighting, particularly a much heavier weight on
implementation cost, changes the winner to the plain graphene torus. The
candidates in Figure~\ref{fig:candidates} are orthographic projections of
parametric surfaces, not photographs, and photographic plausibility is argued
from the optics literature rather than measured. Finally, several citations
that surfaced during the survey --- including a frequently mis-attributed
post-process depth-of-field chapter and a paper on cyclic time in a
multiverse setting --- were discarded because they could not be verified; only
sources whose bibliographic record or DOI was directly retrieved are cited.

\section{Conclusion}

The shipped hero object is a legitimately generated shape: a corner-cut
control polygon, arclength-parameterised, swept along a moving frame, and
given depth by a super-linear ramp with a local occlusion correction. Its
weaknesses are not geometric accidents but consequences of two decisions: the
silhouette was left open, and time was illustrated rather than encoded.

Closing the loop and twisting the cross-section over a rotation-minimising
frame repairs both at once, is provably single-seam, fits the existing
instanced architecture, and keeps the brand mark. The result is the
phase-locked closed G torus, specified in \eqref{eq:helix} and justified in
Table~\ref{tab:semantics}. A plain graphene torus ($k=0$) is the correct
fallback if the twist proves too costly to build.
